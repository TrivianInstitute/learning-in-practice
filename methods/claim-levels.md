# What a field note can support

Use the narrowest claim supported by the available evidence.

| Level | Example wording | What it can support | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Observation | “In one session, learners asked for a second source.” | A reported event in a described setting | Why it happened |
| Interpretation | “The exercise may have prompted source checking.” | A facilitator's plausible reading | Causality or a typical outcome |
| Repeated pattern | “Three reviewed notes report difficulty with the same instruction.” | A reason to revise or investigate | Population prevalence |
| Hypothesis | “A shorter prompt may help new learners separate fact and inference.” | A question for a future test | A tested effect |
| Evaluated finding | Requires a specified design, measures, permissions, comparison or baseline as relevant, and limitations | Only the claim that design and evidence actually warrant | Universal efficacy |

A favorable anecdote does not become an evaluated finding when multiplied or quoted prominently. A negative note can identify a design defect or access barrier without proving that every learner will face it.

## Write with provenance

For each statement, link to the source note, resource version, broad context, date of publication, and any correction. Distinguish what a facilitator saw from what a learner reported and from what the editor inferred. When source notes disagree, keep the disagreement visible.

## Revise claims

If later evidence challenges a synthesis, update it with a dated note and link to the new material. Preserve the history of prior wording. Avoid public counts so granular that individuals or groups become identifiable.

This is a communication framework, not a validated measurement system.
